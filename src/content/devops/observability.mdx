## What observability actually means

**Monitoring** asks predefined questions: "is CPU above 80%?"
**Observability** is the stronger property: can you answer questions
you *didn't* predict — "why are checkouts from Android in Mumbai slow
since the 2pm deploy?" — from the system's outputs alone, without
shipping new code to find out?

The distinction matters because [distributed
systems](/backend/microservices) fail in combinations nobody
pre-imagines. You can't dashboard your way to every answer; you can
instrument your way to *answerable*.

```
Analogy: monitoring is the warning lights on a car's dashboard —
predefined, binary, useful. Observability is being able to plug in
a diagnostic reader and interrogate anything the engine ever did.
```

## The three pillars (and what each is *for*)

### Logs — what happened, in detail

Discrete events with context. The non-negotiable upgrade from
`print` ([Level 1's debugging](/programming/exceptions)) is
**structured logging** — machine-parseable fields, not prose:

```json
{"ts": "2026-06-11T14:02:11Z", "level": "error", "service": "payments",
 "trace_id": "a8f3...", "user_id": 4471, "order_id": 88123,
 "msg": "charge declined", "provider": "stripe", "decline_code": "insufficient_funds"}
```

Prose logs are grep-able; structured logs are *queryable* ("all
declines by provider, last hour, grouped by code"). Rules that pay
rent: log once per event at the right layer
([throw low, catch high, log once](/programming/exceptions)); always
carry the **correlation/trace id** (below); never log secrets or
[PII](/fundamentals/security) (tokens, passwords, card numbers —
log redaction is a compliance control, not a style choice). Logs are
expensive at volume — sample the routine, keep every error.

### Metrics — how much, how fast, numerically

Pre-aggregated numbers over time: cheap to store, fast to query,
ideal for dashboards and alerts. The two acronyms that organize
everything:

- **RED** (per **service**): **R**ate (requests/sec), **E**rrors
  (failure %), **D**uration (latency — as **percentiles**, never
  averages: p50 is the typical user, **p99 is your unlucky-1% user**
  ([the Node doc's metric](/backend/nodejs)), and averages hide
  exactly the pain you're looking for).
- **USE** (per **resource**): **U**tilization, **S**aturation
  (queue depth — [queue depth is destiny](/hld/messaging-and-queues)),
  **E**rrors — for hosts, DBs, pools, brokers.

A RED dashboard per service + USE per shared resource answers "is it
broken and where" in one glance; that's the bar.

### Traces — where the time went, across services

One request, [six services](/backend/microservices): a **distributed
trace** stitches its whole journey into a waterfall of **spans**
(operation, service, start, duration, parent):

```
trace a8f3:  gateway ──────────────────────────── 1,240ms
              └─ orders ────────────────────────  1,180ms
                  ├─ inventory ──                    95ms
                  ├─ payments ───────────────────  1,020ms   ← there it is
                  │    └─ stripe API ───────────     980ms
                  └─ notifications (async)            12ms
```

The mechanism is humble: the gateway mints a **trace id**, and every
hop *propagates it* (HTTP headers, [queue message
headers](/backend/kafka)) while reporting its spans — the standard is
**OpenTelemetry**, the propagation rule is "every client and handler
forwards the context," and the discipline must be universal: one
service that drops the header cuts every trace that crosses it. The
trace id doubles as the **correlation id** that turns six services'
logs into one story ([the microservices doc's
lifeline](/backend/microservices)).

How the pillars compose in a real incident: **alert fires on a metric
→ trace shows which hop → logs of that hop show why.** Three tools,
one workflow — build all three or the workflow has a hole.

## SLOs and error budgets (the management layer)

Observability data becomes *decisions* through three letters:

- **SLI** — a measured indicator of user-visible health: "% of
  checkout requests under 500 ms, successful."
- **SLO** — the target you commit to: "99.9% over 30 days." Chosen
  deliberately — every extra nine multiplies cost
  ([the availability math](/backend/microservices)) and 100% is a
  lie; pick the number where users stop noticing.
- **Error budget** — the allowed failure: 99.9% = ~43 minutes of
  badness per month. **The budget is spendable**: plenty left →
  ship fast, take risks; budget burned → feature work pauses for
  reliability work. This single mechanism converts the eternal
  speed-vs-stability argument into arithmetic — it's SRE's
  ([Google's Site Reliability Engineering practice](/career/engineering-ladder))
  most exportable idea, and naming it well in interviews signals
  operational maturity.

## Alerting philosophy (the part everyone gets wrong)

- **Page on symptoms, not causes:** page when *users hurt* (SLO burn
  rate, error spike, p99 through the ceiling) — not when CPU is high.
  High CPU with healthy latency is Tuesday; paging on it trains
  humans to ignore pages (**alert fatigue** — the failure mode that
  eventually swallows a real incident).
- **Every page must be actionable and urgent.** Not urgent → a
  ticket. Not actionable → a dashboard. The 2 AM test: would you
  *act* on this, right now? No → it doesn't page.
- **Alert on absence too** — "no orders processed in 10 minutes" beats
  every threshold; silence is the failure your error-rate alert
  can't see (the queue consumer that hung without erroring —
  [lag is the metric](/backend/kafka)).
- Burn-rate alerting is the modern default: page when the error
  budget is being consumed too fast (fast burn = page now; slow
  burn = ticket) — it self-calibrates to the SLO instead of needing
  per-metric thresholds.

## Common mistakes

- **Averages on dashboards** — the mean of 99 fast and 1 ten-second
  request looks fine; the percentile screams. p50/p95/p99 or it
  didn't happen.
- **Logging without correlation ids** — six services, six private
  diaries, no story. [This question has no answer
  without it](/backend/microservices).
- **Instrumenting after the incident** — observability is built in
  fair weather; mid-outage is too late
  ([the modular-monolith rule](/backend/ddd): infrastructure you'll
  need is cheapest before you need it).
- **Cardinality explosions** — a metric labeled by user_id creates
  millions of time series and a dead metrics bill; high-cardinality
  questions belong to logs/traces, not metrics labels.
- **Dashboards as wallpaper** — 40 charts nobody reads; one RED
  per service, one USE per resource, one SLO page. Curate or drown.
- **No runbooks** — every alert links to "what to check, what to do";
  a page without a runbook is a riddle at 2 AM.

## Interview perspective

<QA q="Logs vs metrics vs traces — when does each pillar answer the question, and how do they hand off in an incident?">

Metrics: cheap pre-aggregated numbers — detect and quantify ("error
rate 4%, p99 2s, started 14:02") and trigger alerts; they can't tell
you *which request* or *why*. Traces: per-request waterfalls across
services — localize ("the time is in payments' call to Stripe");
they can't aggregate trends and are usually sampled. Logs: full-
context event detail — diagnose ("decline_code:
insufficient_funds, retries exhausted"); too expensive to scan as a
detector. The incident workflow chains them: a metric-based SLO
alert fires → pull traces for the affected route and window, find
the slow/failing span → jump to that service's logs filtered by
trace id for the root cause. The design requirement that makes the
chain work is shared identifiers — the trace id propagated through
every hop and stamped on every log line; break propagation anywhere
and the handoff dies there.

</QA>

<QA q="Define SLI, SLO and error budget, and explain how the budget changes engineering behavior.">

An SLI is a measured user-centric indicator (fraction of requests
fast-and-successful); an SLO is the explicit target for it (99.9%
monthly); the error budget is the complement — the 0.1% (~43
min/month) of allowed failure. The budget reframes reliability from
a virtue into a resource: while budget remains, teams ship
aggressively — deploys, migrations, experiments are *funded* by it;
when burn rate spikes or the budget exhausts, a pre-agreed policy
kicks in — freezes on risky launches, priority to reliability fixes
— no meeting required, because the trade-off was negotiated when the
SLO was set. It also disciplines the target itself: each extra nine
roughly 10×es the cost ([multi-region, consensus, redundancy](/hld/scaling-journey)),
so "how reliable?" becomes a product decision with a price tag
rather than an engineering aspiration. The interview tell: candidates
who've lived this mention burn-rate alerts and the freeze policy;
candidates who've read about it stop at the definitions.

</QA>

<QA q="Design the observability for BookMyShow's booking flow, concretely.">

Instrument the user journey, not just the servers. Metrics: RED per
service (gateway, search, booking, payments,
[notifications](/lld/notification-system)) with p95/p99, plus
domain SLIs — seat-lock success rate, lock-to-payment conversion,
payment-confirm latency; USE for the shared hot spots: the per-show
lock store, [queue depths](/backend/kafka), DB connection pools.
Traces: trace id minted at the gateway, propagated through every
service *and into queue messages*, so an async confirmation email
still joins its booking's story; span the external payment call
separately — it's the latency you don't control. Logs: structured,
with trace_id, show_id and booking_id on every line; full detail on
every failed lock and payment decline, sampled success logs. SLOs:
"99.9% of seat-lock requests under 300 ms, successful" and "99.95%
of paid bookings confirmed within 10 s" — with burn-rate paging and
an absence alert ("zero completed bookings in 5 min during open
hours" — the one that catches the silent hang). Runbook links on
every page. The structure to show off: instrument the *invariants
and funnels the business cares about*, then the resources they ride
on.

</QA>

## Practice

1. **Structure your logs:** take any practice API from
   [Level 7](/backend/nodejs) and convert prints to structured JSON
   logs with a request-scoped trace id middleware; verify one
   request's story is one `grep`.
2. **RED in 30 minutes:** add Prometheus counters/histograms
   (requests, errors, duration) and a three-panel dashboard. Break
   the service; watch the panels tell you before the user does.
3. **Trace a chain:** two services calling each other with
   OpenTelemetry auto-instrumentation + Jaeger; find a deliberately
   slow downstream in the waterfall.
4. **Write one SLO:** for your project, define one SLI, pick a
   defensible target, compute the monthly budget in minutes, and
   write the two alerts (fast burn, slow burn) with their runbook
   stubs.

**Level 10 is complete.** Observability is the lens for everything
else you've built — next on the [roadmap](/roadmap): the remaining
database internals and AI foundations.
