## Prompt

> Design ride-hailing: riders request a trip, nearby drivers are
> matched, both sides track each other live, pricing responds to
> demand. Millions of concurrent drivers and riders.

## 1. Requirements

**Functional:** rider requests with pickup/destination; match to a
nearby available driver; live location tracking both ways; trip
lifecycle (request → match → pickup → ride → payment); dynamic
("surge") pricing per area.
**Non-functional:** matching in a few seconds; location freshness of a
few seconds; **a trip must never be double-assigned or lost**
(correctness over availability for the match itself); city-scale
locality (Mumbai's traffic never needs Chicago's data).

The opening move: *"Unlike the
[feed](/hld/design-instagram)/[video](/hld/design-youtube) systems,
the hard data here is a **continuously moving geospatial index** plus
a **transactional matching** step. I'll deep-dive those two."*

## 2. Estimation (shapes the design)

~5 M drivers online at peak, each phone reporting location every ~4 s
→ **~1.25 M location writes/sec** — the single biggest stream in the
system, and it's *ephemeral* (only the latest few points matter).
Trip requests: ~1 M trips/hour peak ≈ ~300 matches/sec — tiny by
comparison, but each one is a small distributed transaction. The
asymmetry **is** the architecture: a firehose of disposable writes
next to a trickle of precious ones.

## 4. Deep dives

### The geospatial index ("find drivers near me")

The naive query — `WHERE distance(driver, rider) < 2km` — computes
distance to every driver per request. The fix is the same move as
every index ([B-trees](/dsa/trees-and-bsts),
[hashes](/dsa/hash-tables)): **precompute structure that narrows the
search**. For geography, divide the world into **cells**:

- **Geohash / S2 / H3** — encode (lat, lng) into a cell id where
  nearby points share prefixes (geohash) or neighboring ids (S2/H3
  hexagons). The index becomes an ordinary
  [hash map](/dsa/hash-tables): `cell id → set of driver ids`.
- "Nearby drivers" = look up the rider's cell **plus its 8
  neighbors** (the neighbor lookup is why purpose-built geo cells beat
  naive grid squares at the poles/meridians — say "H3" and move on).
- A driver's 4-second update: remove from old cell's set, add to new —
  two O(1) operations on an in-memory store
  ([Redis-class](/hld/caching)), sharded by region. The
  quadtree/k-d-tree alternative earns a mention for static points
  ("find restaurants"), but **cells beat trees when everything moves**
  — constant-time updates, no rebalancing.

Durability stance worth stating: locations are **deliberately not
durable** — a crashed index shard rebuilds within seconds from the
next wave of updates ([the same "derived data" argument](/hld/design-instagram)
as feed caches). Only *trips* are precious.

### Matching (the transactional trickle)

1. Rider requests → matching service queries the geo index → ranks
   candidates (distance/ETA, driver rating, acceptance rate).
2. **Offer** the trip to the top driver with a ~15 s TTL — the
   [seat-lock pattern](/lld/bookmyshow) wearing a seatbelt: the offer
   *reserves* the driver (state: `OFFERED`), so they're excluded from
   other matches; expiry releases them automatically.
3. Accept → atomic check-and-set on the trip (`PENDING → MATCHED`,
   driver `AVAILABLE → ON_TRIP`). Two matching services racing for
   one driver is the [double-booking race](/lld/bookmyshow); the
   driver's state transition is the serialization point.
4. Decline/timeout → next candidate. Nobody available → expand radius,
   then surge (below).

The **trip state machine** (`REQUESTED → MATCHED → ARRIVING →
IN_PROGRESS → COMPLETED/CANCELLED`) lives in a durable, replicated
store — this is the system's [BookMyShow booking](/lld/bookmyshow) /
[order saga](/backend/microservices): every transition is an event,
payments hang off `COMPLETED`, and both apps render *the same machine*.

### Live tracking during a trip

Rider watches the car crawl toward them: the driver's location stream
is *routed* — during a trip, updates also push to the paired rider
over the existing [WebSocket machinery](/backend/websockets) (session
registry says where the rider's socket lives, [the WhatsApp routing
table](/hld/design-whatsapp)). Client-side smoothing/map-snapping makes
4-second updates look continuous — a *frontend* trick saving 4× the
backend load, worth one sentence in any interview.

### Surge pricing

Per cell, per minute: demand (open requests) vs supply (available
drivers) → a multiplier, computed by a streaming job over the two
firehoses ([the windowed-aggregation pattern](/hld/design-youtube)
again — it's everywhere). Published to riders before they request.
Two honest notes: it's a *control system* (the multiplier exists to
move supply toward demand), and it's lag-tolerant — a 60-second-stale
multiplier is fine, which is why it's a streaming aggregate and not a
synchronous query.

## 5. Bottlenecks & failure modes

- **Geo-index shard dies** → that city's matching degrades for
  seconds; index rebuilds from live updates. Riders retry; trips
  in-flight are unaffected (they live in the durable store). This
  failure story — *ephemeral layer crashes are non-events* — is the
  payoff of the durability split.
- **Event spike** (concert ends, rain starts) → request volume × 10 in
  one cell: matching queue grows, radius expansion + surge are the
  *designed* relief valves; the geo index itself doesn't care.
- **Offer storms on the best driver** — serialization on driver state
  prevents double-assignment; ranking diversity (don't offer everyone
  the same top driver) prevents convoy effects.
- **Cross-city is embarrassingly shardable** — Mumbai and Delhi share
  nothing at runtime; regional isolation is both a scaling and a
  blast-radius strategy ([cellular architecture](/hld/scalability)).

<QA q="How do you index millions of moving drivers for 'who's near me' queries — and why not a quadtree or PostGIS?">

Divide space into cells (geohash prefixes or hex systems like H3);
maintain an in-memory map of cell → driver set, sharded by region. A
location update is two O(1) set operations (leave old cell, join new);
a proximity query reads the rider's cell plus neighbors and ranks the
few hundred candidates. Quadtrees/k-d trees answer range queries
beautifully for *static* data, but millions of moves/sec mean constant
restructure — cells make updates structural no-ops. A disk-backed
PostGIS handles the *durable* geo data (city boundaries, places), not
a 1.25 M write/sec ephemeral stream; the live index belongs in memory,
rebuilt-on-crash from the stream itself. Close with the H3-hexagon
note (uniform neighbor distances, no pole distortion) — it signals
you've met real geo systems, not just the textbook.

</QA>

<QA q="Walk through the matching flow and how you prevent one driver being assigned two trips.">

Query the geo index for candidates near the pickup; rank by ETA and
quality signals; offer to the top candidate with a 15-second TTL,
atomically transitioning the driver to OFFERED so concurrent matches
exclude them — the reservation IS the lock, and the TTL makes
abandoned offers self-release (the BookMyShow seat-lock pattern). On
accept, a conditional update commits driver → ON_TRIP and trip →
MATCHED; the conditional ("only if still OFFERED to this trip") is the
serialization point — two racing matchers, one winner, the loser moves
to the next candidate. All trip transitions live in a durable
replicated store, because trips are the precious data. The follow-up
to pre-empt: batching — at high request density you match *batches*
of requests to *batches* of drivers (an assignment problem) rather
than greedily one-by-one, trading a second of latency for globally
better ETAs. Naming that greedy-vs-batch trade is the senior close.

</QA>

<QA q="Driver location updates are 1.25M writes/sec but you said they're 'not durable'. Defend that decision.">

Durability has a cost — replication, fsync, ordering — and it buys you
the ability to *recover the data after a crash*. But a driver location
is stale in 4 seconds by definition: after any crash, the recovered
data would be useless before recovery finished, because the next
update wave rebuilds the entire index organically within seconds. So
durability would purchase nothing. The system keeps the live index in
replicated *memory* (replicas for availability, not durability), and
persists only two derived artifacts: trip-attached location traces
(for receipts, support, safety — written asynchronously to cheap
storage) and aggregate heatmaps for surge/analytics. The principle to
generalize: **classify data by its useful lifetime — ephemeral,
derived, or precious — and pay durability costs only for precious.**
The same logic justified [Instagram's rebuildable feed
cache](/hld/design-instagram); recognizing it as one principle across
designs is what staff-level answers sound like.

</QA>
