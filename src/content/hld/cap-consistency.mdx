## CAP, stated correctly

A distributed store can't simultaneously guarantee all three of **C**onsistency,
**A**vailability, **P**artition-tolerance. Since network partitions *will* happen,
P isn't optional — so the real choice **during a partition** is:

- **CP** — refuse some requests to stay consistent (e.g. a banking ledger).
- **AP** — keep serving, accept that replicas may diverge and reconcile later
  (e.g. a social feed, a shopping cart).

<Callout type="warn" title="CAP is a partition-time choice, not an always-on label">
When the network is healthy you can have both consistency and availability. CAP
only bites *during* a partition. PACELC extends it: **E**lse (no partition),
you still trade **L**atency vs **C**onsistency — strong consistency costs round
trips.
</Callout>

## The consistency spectrum

| Model | Guarantee | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Strong | every read sees the latest write | balances, inventory decrement |
| Read-your-writes | you see your own updates immediately | edit your profile |
| Monotonic reads | you never see time go backwards | a paginated feed |
| Eventual | replicas converge "soon" | view counts, social timelines |

Most large systems are **eventually consistent** by default and reserve strong
consistency for the few operations that truly need it.

## ACID vs BASE

- **ACID** (classic RDBMS): Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability —
  transactions are all-or-nothing and isolated. Great for money, orders,
  anything with invariants. StockStump's balance decrement needs this.
- **BASE** (many NoSQL): Basically Available, Soft state, Eventually consistent —
  trades strict guarantees for availability and scale.

The decision isn't ideological — it's per-workload. A trading balance is ACID; a
"who viewed your profile" counter is happily BASE.

<QA q="State the CAP theorem and what it means for a design decision.">

During a network partition you can't have both consistency and availability, so
you pick: CP (reject requests to avoid serving stale/divergent data) or AP (keep
serving and reconcile later). Since partitions are unavoidable, every
distributed datastore is effectively choosing CP or AP for its failure behavior.
In an interview I'd tie it to the data: money/inventory → CP; feeds/counters →
AP.

</QA>

<QA q="Give an example where eventual consistency is fine and one where it isn't.">

Fine: a tweet's like-count or "X people are viewing this" — if it's stale for a
few seconds nobody is harmed, and AP availability is more valuable. Not fine: a
bank balance or seat/inventory reservation — a stale read lets two users spend
or book the same thing, which is a correctness bug. Those need strong
consistency (a transaction or an atomic conditional update).

</QA>

<QA q="Why not make everything strongly consistent to be safe?">

Strong consistency costs latency and availability: it needs coordination
(consensus/round trips) and must reject writes during partitions. Forcing it
everywhere makes the whole system slower and less available than its weakest
guarantee needs to be. You scope strong consistency to the operations with hard
invariants and let the rest be eventually consistent.

</QA>
